# Weld defects: causes and fixes

This is general welding guidance for visual inspection. Always follow the welding procedure specification (WPS) and the acceptance standard that applies to the job.

# Porosity

Gas pores or holes trapped in the weld metal, seen as small round cavities, alone or in clusters.
- Common causes: poor shielding gas coverage (flow too low or too high, draughts, blocked nozzle), contaminated base metal (oil, paint, rust, moisture), damp electrodes or wire, too long an arc.
- Fixes: check gas flow and nozzle, shield the area from wind, clean the joint back to bright metal, use dry consumables, keep a consistent arc length. Grind out and re-weld porous sections that exceed the acceptance limit.

# Cracks

Breaks in the weld or the heat-affected zone. They can run along the weld (longitudinal), across it (transverse) or appear at the end of a bead (crater cracks).
- Common causes: high restraint, fast cooling, hydrogen in the weld, wrong filler metal, too small a weld for the joint, filling craters poorly.
- Fixes: preheat where required, control cooling, use low-hydrogen consumables, match filler to base metal, fill craters at the end of each run.
- Cracks are usually rejectable under every quality level. A cracked weld must be removed and re-welded, and the cause found.

# Undercut

A groove melted into the base metal along the toe of the weld that is left unfilled.
- Common causes: current too high, travel speed too fast, wrong electrode angle, arc too long.
- Fixes: lower the current, slow down, correct the angle, and add a repair pass where the depth exceeds the limit.

# Lack of fusion

Weld metal that has not properly fused with the base metal or with a previous pass.
- Common causes: current too low, travel too fast, wrong angle, poor joint preparation, contaminated surfaces.
- Fixes: increase heat input within the procedure, correct the torch angle, clean between passes, open up the joint if access is poor. Unfused sections must be ground out and re-welded.

# Incomplete penetration

The weld does not reach through the full thickness of the joint (at the root).
- Common causes: root gap too small, current too low, travel too fast, wrong joint design.
- Fixes: correct the root gap and preparation, increase current within the procedure, back-gouge and weld from the other side where possible.

# Spatter

Small droplets of metal thrown out of the arc that stick to the surface beside the weld.
- Common causes: current or voltage set wrong, long arc, poor gas, dirty surface.
- Fixes: adjust settings, shorten the arc, check gas, clean the surface. Spatter is usually cosmetic but should be removed before coating.

# Burn-through

A hole melted right through the base metal, usually on thin material or at the root.
- Common causes: too much heat, too slow travel, too wide a root gap.
- Fixes: reduce current, travel faster, tighten the gap, use a backing bar. Repair the hole before continuing; on pressure parts the joint normally needs repair and re-testing.

# Overlap

Weld metal that flows onto the surface of the base metal without fusing to it.
- Common causes: travel too slow, current too low, wrong angle.
- Fixes: increase travel speed or current within the procedure, correct the angle, grind off the overlap.

# Rust and corrosion

Rust (iron oxide) forms on steel exposed to moisture and oxygen. Near welds it matters for two reasons: rust on the joint before welding causes porosity and lack of fusion, and corrosion on finished welds and nearby metal weakens the structure over time.
- Before welding: clean the joint back to bright metal.
- On finished welds: measure the extent, remove loose rust, and assess whether the remaining wall thickness is still acceptable. Heavy or spreading corrosion, especially on pressure equipment and pipelines, should be reported and assessed by a qualified inspector.

# Acceptance standards

Acceptance limits depend on the standard used for the job. Two common ones:
- **ISO 5817** defines quality levels for fusion-welded joints in steel: B (stringent), C (intermediate) and D (moderate), with limits for each type of imperfection.
- **AWS D1.1** is the American Welding Society structural welding code for steel.
The WeldSight assistant can explain these in general terms, but the exact limits must be taken from the current edition of the standard itself.
